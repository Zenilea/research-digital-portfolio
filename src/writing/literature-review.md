Three-dimensional (3D) printing has been proposed as a way for hospitals in low- and middle-income countries to produce equipment and spare parts locally when supplies run short (Fleischer et al., 2020). Most of this work relies on fused deposition modeling (FDM), in which a printer builds a part by laying down lines of melted plastic one layer at a time. Printed polylactic acid (PLA) instruments have already shown promise: Rankin et al. (2014) printed an Army-Navy retractor that held 13.6 kg of force before breaking. A reusable instrument, however, must also be cleaned between uses. Because FDM parts contain small gaps and voids between printed lines (Özer, 2025), cleaning raises two separate concerns: whether the plastic survives the cleaning process, and whether the cleaning chemicals soak into the part and stay there.

## Sterilization and Disinfection of Printed PLA

Sterilization and disinfection are not interchangeable. Alcohol, for example, kills bacteria most effectively at 60 to 90% concentration, but it is not recommended for sterilizing surgical materials because it does not destroy bacterial spores (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [CDC], 2023). Studies on heat consistently show that steam sterilization, the standard hospital method, damages PLA (Chen et al., 2020; Rynio et al., 2022). Chen et al. (2020) found that autoclaving reduced a PLA retractor's failure load from 95 N to 15.7 N, and Rynio et al. (2022) found that steam at 121 °C badly deformed PLA templates even though it eliminated every microorganism tested.

Chemical disinfectants, by contrast, appear far gentler on printed PLA (Fleischer et al., 2020; Rankin et al., 2014; Vaňková et al., 2020). Rankin et al. (2014) found no loss of strength after glutaraldehyde, and Fleischer et al. (2020) found that seven days or 25 short cycles in Cidex OPA or chlorine raised median strength by 12.5%, with a largest decrease of only 8.8%. Vaňková et al. (2020) showed that 70% isopropyl alcohol, along with ethanol and a hypochlorite disinfectant, completely removed applied bacteria and SARS-CoV-2 from PLA without major surface changes. However, the CDC (2023) warns that repeated alcohol use can swell and harden some plastics, and Fleischer et al.'s repeated cycles used Cidex OPA and chlorine rather than alcohol. Therefore, chemical disinfection is the more realistic option for printed PLA, but the effect of repeated alcohol exposure specifically remains uncertain.

## Porosity and Surface Sealing

Even when the plastic survives, the disinfectant may not leave it. Fleischer et al. (2020) found that a third of their treatments increased sample weight by up to 8.3%, while the samples with the best surface quality gained nothing, and concluded that surface porosity must be minimized to keep disinfectant from transferring to people. Vaňková et al. (2020) appear to disagree, describing printed PLA as compact enough to block microorganism-sized particles. Their test, however, measured particles passing through a mask rather than liquid entering a part, so the two studies answer different questions. Changing the filament's chemistry has not proven to be a reliable fix either: Kiel et al. (2022) found that bacteria attached to both standard PLA and a nano-copper "antimicrobial" PLA, with the copper slowing only one of the three species tested.

A physical approach, sealing the surface, has been more promising. Several studies have found that coatings or infiltrated resins fill voids and reduce water uptake in printed parts (Narayan et al., 2023; Özer, 2025; Shen et al., 2021). Shen et al. (2021) brushed Smooth-On XTC-3D, the epoxy selected for this study, onto printed ABS and found lower moisture uptake with little change in tensile strength. However, the coating began separating from the plastic at a relatively low strain, suggesting that a tool that flexes during use could crack its own seal. Therefore, while sealing appears to reduce absorption, its durability is uncertain, and none of these studies tested a sealed PLA part through repeated disinfection.
